Business analysis playbook

One loop, three ways in

Most business work runs on a loop: plan, do, check, act. A business intelligence analyst supports every turn of it: agreeing targets and definitions, tracking the numbers, explaining what moved, and helping the teams who act.

The analyst's work usually starts in one of three ways: a KPI they own moves, another team asks a question, or a team starts an initiative.

These notes come from work in companies with several teams, a ticketing system and regular business reviews. In a small business the same loop applies with lighter tools: a shared sheet instead of tickets, a weekly catch-up instead of a formal review.

The example: a café chain and its monthly orders

Imagine a made-up chain of 20 coffee shops. Customers order in six ways: delivery apps, takeaway, dine-in, app pre-orders, catering and the drive-through.

The metric followed is monthly orders, about 124k last October.

A key metric gets an owner, a target and one definition

Monthly orders is owned by the BI analyst, who tracks it, explains its moves and works with the teams who act.

Before the month starts, the target is agreed with the business-unit head, and every team counts an order the same way.

Weekly tracking shows orders slipping

During the month, the analyst checks orders every week against target and last year.

Through September and October, orders fall a little further each week while last year's line holds steady.

October closes 5.8k orders down

At the monthly review, October shows 118.2k orders against 124.0k a year earlier: −4.7%, and 3.8k below target.

As the metric's owner, the analyst is expected to explain why.

First, the analyst makes sure the drop is real

A drop in the report isn't always a real drop: late or incomplete data can make orders look lower than they were.

That's why it's checked first: the same definition both years, complete data, and totals that match the master dashboard.

Then the drop is broken down, with the AI writing the query

To see where the drop came from, the analyst and the AI first agree what to count and how. The AI then writes the SQL, and the analyst checks the output before reading anything into it.

Four channels lost orders, and two gained

Split by how customers order, each row compares this October with last. The red bars are the losses.

Delivery apps explain 83% of the drop

Delivery-app orders changed by −4.8k, most of the 5.8k drop. One large channel holds most of the answer, so that's where action will pay back.

The next questions: which shops and which apps, and does the operations team's explanation hold up in the data?

The drive-through fell fastest, but it's small

By rate, drive-through orders changed by −35%, yet that's only 0.7k orders. It's noted to revisit later rather than made the headline.

The analysis stops when more detail wouldn't change the action.

Before anyone acts, the business-unit head tests the finding

The head brings context the data doesn't have and asks questions. The analyst answers each one with data, until the finding holds or is dismissed.

The operations manager turns the finding into an action

A shared thread brings in the evidence, priority and timeline. The operations manager designs a delivery-app promotion, and the analyst supplies data as the plan takes shape.

Agreeing on a cause doesn't prove it, so the data keeps backing each step.

At the business review, the slide leads with the finding

Before the meeting, documents are updated by the cutoff, the numbers pre-reviewed with the head, and presenters agreed.

On the slide, the title states the finding and the chart proves it.

Next month's tracking shows whether it worked

Once the promotion starts, weekly tracking shows whether orders recover. Either way, the result feeds the next plan, and the loop turns again.

orders_by_channel_change.sql
-- Monthly orders by channel, October 2026 vs October 2025
-- Illustrative schema: one row per order
WITH monthly AS (
  SELECT
    order_channel,
    order_month,
    COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS orders
  FROM cafe.orders
  WHERE is_refunded = FALSE
    AND is_staff_order = FALSE
    AND order_month IN ('2025-10-01', '2026-10-01')
  GROUP BY order_channel, order_month
),
pivoted AS (
  SELECT
    order_channel,
    SUM(CASE WHEN order_month = '2025-10-01' THEN orders ELSE 0 END) AS last_year,
    SUM(CASE WHEN order_month = '2026-10-01' THEN orders ELSE 0 END) AS this_year
  FROM monthly
  GROUP BY order_channel
)
SELECT
  order_channel,
  last_year,
  this_year,
  this_year - last_year AS change,
  ROUND(100.0 * (this_year - last_year) / NULLIF(last_year, 0), 1) AS change_pct
FROM pivoted
ORDER BY change;

When another team asks

Requests come in through a ticket on a short template. Here is how one moves, from before it's written to after it closes, with notes beside the fields that matter most.

  1. 1
    Often the answer already exists

    Before any work starts, existing dashboards and past outputs come first. If one answers the question and the requester is happy with it, the work stops there.

  2. 2
    The ticket is filled in on the template

    The two notes mark the fields that shape the work.

    Data request template

    Requesting team
    Which team the request is for
    Objective
    What are you trying to understand?
    Description
    What makes you want to understand it?
    Next course of action
    What will you do with the output?

    What happens next decides how deep to dig. This field says what the requester will do with the answer. It sets the depth of the analysis, and ranks the request against other work by its likely impact.

    Example output
    Link to a mock-up sheet of the output you expect. Required.
    Criteria
    The filters, such as which items and which date period. Say whether you need it on a recurring basis.
    Dimensions
    The columns, starting with granularity: daily, weekly or monthly.
    Metrics
    The values you'd like to track.
    Special criteria
    Anything else to consider.
    Deadline
    3, 5, 7 or 10 working days, weekends excluded.

    The date is agreed, not just requested. It's confirmed in the next step and written back into the ticket.

  3. 3
    A written agreement saves the argument later

    Anything unclear gets settled in a quick chat, and the understanding and due date go into the ticket. If the date doesn't work, the requester takes it to the manager, so priorities are decided openly.

  4. 4
    A ticket closes once someone has acted

    The output goes out with notes on filters and definitions. A week later comes a follow-up: was it used, and what action was taken? Then the ticket closes. Expanded scope gets a new ticket linked to the first.
