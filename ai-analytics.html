AI in analytics

About 5 minutes to read

How the ideas in this guide come together in data analysis: where AI fits in my workflow, the two places I check its work, and what a team should see before relying on an AI agent.

Two checkpoints keep the analysis trustworthy

This chapter is for people who work with data, so a few terms are worth a line each. SQL is the language used to pull data out of databases. A join combines two tables, and the grain is the level of detail of each row, such as one row per order or one row per day. Metadata is information about the data itself, such as what each column means.

In my analysis work, AI drafts and runs a lot of the work, but two checkpoints never move. The AI never interprets data I haven't validated, and nothing goes out in its words alone. You can see the workflow in the playbook story, where the AI writes the query that splits the drop in orders by channel.

  1. Me and the AIAgree the plan

    Objective, output, tables, joins, filters, grain and calculations. It draws on past SQL, table metadata, metric definitions, business context, team rules and data-privacy standards.

  2. The AIWrite and run the SQL

    Within the plan. Any material change comes back to me for realignment.

  3. First checkpoint, meValidate the raw output

    Columns and filters against the request, tricky calculated fields checked, totals reconciled.

  4. The AIExplore and draft

    Exploratory analysis, a first interpretation and charts, each citing its source.

  5. Second checkpoint, meReview the reasoning

    Do the insights follow from the verified output and the business context?

  6. MeFinal interpretation

    The conclusions and visuals are mine. The AI helps rephrase or build slides.

  7. Me and the AIArchive for reuse

    Plan, SQL, checks, output and assumptions saved together, so the work can be rerun.

Every insight the AI drafts should point to its evidence: the raw output I verified, and any business context or earlier analysis it used. When it relies on something from the internet instead, I check that before it shapes a recommendation, as chapter 3 describes.

Organise the context so AI can find it

The plan is only as good as what the AI can read while making it: past SQL, table descriptions, metric definitions, business context, my preferences for SQL, the team's rules and the standards for sensitive data. Organising that context is ongoing work.

  • Team-agreed information belongs in shared tools the whole team can reach, such as a data catalogue, a code repository or a shared drive.
  • Personal preferences and notes stay local or in my own repository, along with some business context specific to my role.
  • Conflicts get raised. When the AI finds two sources that disagree, it flags them for me to settle.

When the AI gets something wrong because it lacked context, I add that context to what it reads next time. Improvements to shared context are worth prioritising by their effect on the quality of the work and on the team's ability to use AI.

In my practice

While working on SQL, data and dashboards, I watch for tables that seem poorly structured or heavy to query. When a planned script gets long and complex, I ask the AI for better models or layers where it could be optimised, and I add that preference to the context for future planning.

Check what's changed before reusing old work

The last step of the workflow saves everything together: the plan, the SQL, the checks, the outputs, the references and assumptions, a summary of the instructions, the ticket and the final version I edited. That archive makes reruns quick, and it's useful for periodic reviews, related requests and changes of format.

A query that was right last quarter isn't automatically right now. Before rerunning old work, compare the period, conditions, assumptions and definitions with the new request.

Analysts need the foundations to judge AI's work

AI can write the query and draft the chart, but someone has to judge whether they're right. That takes three kinds of knowledge:

  • Business context: what the business and its teams are trying to achieve.
  • Metrics: how each one is defined and what moves it.
  • Basic SQL and data knowledge: enough to read a query, spot a wrong join or filter, and reconcile a total.

These can be learnt alongside AI. Learn through actual tasks with AI, using the learning habits in chapter 2, and the foundations grow with the work.

Start a team on real requests

For a team trying an analytics agent, the place to start is the requests analysts already handle: data, analysis and dashboard requests, using the definitions the team has agreed, with the output validated. Then show the people who'll rely on it what it does on real work they can judge. Four things should be visible before use expands:

  • the data is accurate;
  • every number, fact and source in the output is real and can be traced, with nothing invented (what's often called hallucination);
  • the results are logical;
  • it saves real time.

These are the checks I'd want a team to see, not results from a pilot. Prove it on real requests before expanding.

My view: how AI should work in a company

This is my perspective on where AI at work should go, not a project I'm running. I see a chat-based agent, perhaps starting as a few focused bots, that eases the steps of everyday analytics work: getting data and insights, building monitoring dashboards, summarising meetings, following up on projects with their context, and helping with planning.

Its value would come from context: connections to the team's communication, documentation, project and operational tools, with access managed by team and by need. Further ahead, an agent could also check its own numbers against the documented source of truth, as automation improves. The checks in the section above would apply to it.

Check your understanding

The AI has written and run the SQL, and its first chart looks convincing. What has to happen before it interprets anything?

You validate the raw output: columns and filters against the request, tricky calculated fields, and totals reconciled with a trusted reference.

Last quarter's query answered a similar request. Can you rerun it unchanged for this quarter's request?

Not without checking. First compare the period, conditions, assumptions and definitions with the new request; any of them may have changed.

A team wants to start using an analytics agent. What should it see before using it more widely?

Real requests handled with the team's agreed definitions, and outputs that are accurate, have no invented numbers, facts or sources, are logical and save real time.
