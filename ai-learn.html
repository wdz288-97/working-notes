Using AI, chapter 1 of 6

Learn what AI can do

About 12 minutes to read

This chapter starts from zero: what AI chat apps are, how to try them on small tasks, how they got here, and how to keep learning and choose tools that suit you. You don't need to know anything about AI to begin.

Key ideas

  • AI chat apps are fluent and quick, but they can be confidently wrong, and they don't know your situation unless you tell them.
  • Start with small, real tasks, and keep private information out.
  • In five years, AI has grown from making suggestions to remembering, seeing and carrying out tasks, and from text alone to images, sound and video.
  • Learn a little of everything, keep up through people who use AI, and judge tools by real work.
  • Rankings help you shortlist; a small trial on your own work tells you what fits.

AI chat apps are fluent, fast and sometimes wrong

An AI chat app, such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or DeepSeek, is a program you talk to in everyday language. You type or say what you want, and it replies. Behind it is a large language model: a system trained on huge amounts of text to predict what comes next, word by word. That simple-sounding skill lets it explain, summarise, translate, draft, brainstorm and write code.

Three things are worth knowing from the start:

  • It's fluent. Answers read well and arrive in seconds, on almost any subject.
  • It can be confidently wrong. A model can state something false in the same smooth tone as something true, which is often called hallucination.1 Check anything that matters.
  • It only knows about you what you tell it. It doesn't know your situation, your goals or your standards until you explain them. Chapter 2 is about doing that well.

Think of it as a capable new colleague: quick and knowledgeable, but new to your work.

Start with small, real tasks

The best way to learn is to use it on small things you'd do anyway. Pick one chat app; most have a free version. Then try three kinds of task:

  • Explain something. "Explain how compound interest works, with an example, in plain words." If the answer is too technical, ask for it more simply.
  • Improve something you wrote. Paste a message or an email and ask for a clearer, shorter version. Keep what you like and say what to change.
  • Plan something. A trip, a week of meals or a study plan. Give it your limits, such as budget, time and preferences, and ask for a first draft.

Notice where it saves you time and where it gets things wrong. Treat the first answer as a draft, and reply with what to change.

Common trap: pasting private information

Don't put passwords, ID or bank numbers, other people's personal details or confidential work data into a chat app. Before using one for work, check its settings and your employer's rules.

In my practice

Before working on a task, I always ask myself what its steps are and which of them AI can help with. I use AI across my work and personal life, for research, learning, writing and coding, while I review the important outputs and make the important decisions myself.

Try this

This week, use a chat app for one real task of each kind above. For each, note one thing it did well and one thing you had to fix.

AI has grown from answering to doing

AI didn't arrive all at once. In about five years it has grown from making suggestions to carrying out whole tasks:

WhenWhat changedAn everyday example
2021AI began helping inside tools people already useProgrammers got suggestions as they typed code2
2022Anyone could chat with AIChatGPT launched in November, free in a web browser3
2024AI could remember, see and listenIt remembers that you're vegetarian when suggesting recipes,4 or answers questions about a photo you take5
2025AI started carrying out tasksDescribe what you want built, and an agent writes the code, tests it and fixes it, step by step67
2026Assistants started working in the backgroundAn assistant keeps going on a task, such as tracking prices or preparing a summary, while you get on with your day89

An agent, in this guide, is AI that takes actions to finish a task: searching, writing files, running code or using apps. Each new ability adds to the older ones; chat didn't disappear when agents arrived.

The kinds of media AI works with have widened too. The first chat apps handled text only. Since then, AI has learned to create images from a description,10 understand photos and speech,5 and make video11 and music.12 A model that works with more than one kind of media, such as text, images and sound, is called multimodal. Knowing what's possible today helps you see where AI could help you.

The model is only one part of the app

The app you use is more than its model. Around the model, the product adds parts that change what it can do, so the same model can perform differently in different apps.13

  • Tools let it act: search the web, run code, use a computer.
  • A workspace holds the files it works on, what it's allowed to do, and a record of what it did. Engineers call this the harness.13
  • Memory carries notes from one conversation to the next, like a notebook that sometimes needs correcting.14
  • Skills are saved instructions for a kind of task, which the AI loads when they're relevant.15
  • Connections link it to other apps, such as your calendar or email. A common standard for this is the Model Context Protocol (MCP), from late 2024.16
  • Your documents can be looked up when needed, so the AI answers from them instead of from what it learnt in training alone. This is called retrieval.17

When a result disappoints, the model is just one of the parts to suspect. Chapter 3 lists the others.

Learn a little of everything, then practise

Once you've tried it, a broad map helps you decide what to learn next. Learn a little of everything, keep it in mind, and go deeper as you use it:

  1. How it works, roughly. It reads and writes in small chunks of text called tokens, and it can only hold so much at once, which is its context window. Many models also work with images, sound and video, which is called multimodal.
  2. Where it's used. The jobs and industries already using AI, which gives you a feel for what it can do.
  3. The main apps. Chat apps such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, DeepSeek, Doubao, Qwen and Kimi; coding helpers; and website builders such as Lovable. Some companies build their own models and some use other companies' models, and prices differ.
  4. How to work with it. Writing a good request (the prompt), giving it the right background (the context), and checking its work round by round (the loop). Chapters 2 and 3 cover these.
  5. What lets it act. Skills, connections to other apps, plugins and hooks: the parts that let an agent use tools and repeat a method.
  6. Optional depth. How models are trained, and the research the main AI companies publish. It's interesting, but you can work well with AI without it.

Then practise, and keep learning as you go.

Keep up through regular users and the AI companies

AI apps change every few months, so where you learn matters as much as what you learn. Two kinds of source work well together:

  • People who use AI every day, such as content creators and practitioners, show real use and give quick feedback on new releases, including whether the rankings hold up in practice.
  • The official accounts of the AI companies, both those that build models and those that build apps, tell you what's new and what has changed.

Most learning still comes from use. People who use AI often know a trick you don't, and explaining AI to someone who doesn't use it shows you where your own understanding is thin.

In my practice

I learn from reading posts on X and from constantly using AI in my personal life and at work. I also talk with people who use AI, to see if there's anything new to me, and with people who don't, such as friends and colleagues, because I learn by teaching.

Learn the method behind a demo

A demo, an expert's article or a social media thread may show you something you'd never have thought of: rehearsing a difficult conversation, making practice questions for a class, or an agent turning a short description into a working website. Treat these as invitations to investigate. An expert's result doesn't mean the same approach will work as well for you.

A reputation gets a job candidate an interview; a relevant work sample shows whether they fit. When someone says an AI is excellent at a job, look for the task, what it was given, the mistakes, the corrections and the finished result. A screenshot of a polished result says little about whether it's right, or how long it took to fix.

Look for the decisions behind the demonstration. Why did the person give it that information? Why did they do one step themselves? What mistake did their check catch? Ask what this person knows, supplies or checks that makes the method work. An expert may hand a step to AI because they can spot subtle errors in it; your version may need smaller steps or an extra check.

Common trap: copying the prompt

The wording of an expert's prompt is the least important part of their method. The information they supplied and the checks they kept are what made it work.

Read AI rankings with care

A benchmark is a standard test used to compare AI models, and leaderboards rank models by their scores. A ranking invites one question: who's first? A more useful one is: what exactly was tested? Like any statistic, the headline number depends on what was measured and how.

What it testsExampleWhat it doesn't tell you
Hard knowledge questionsGPQA18Whether it's competent in your field
Fixing real codeSWE-bench19Whether it finishes your whole project
Using a computerOSWorld20Whether it acts safely and correctly in every app
What people preferArena, where people compare two answers side by side21Whether the answers are factually right
Speed and priceArtificial Analysis22Your own time spent checking and fixing

Before trusting a ranking, check which version of the model was tested, what tools it had, and how many tries it took. A result after several tries says little about getting it right first time.23 A ranking helps you shortlist; trying it on your own work tells you whether it fits.

In my practice

I check Artificial Analysis to see which models are good at what, and I follow practitioners to see whether ranking results hold up in real use. When a ranking or other users disagree with my experience of a tool, I try it with and without their feedback before disagreeing. If I still disagree, I keep their view in mind, carry on with my current tool, and research the real use cases behind it.

Run a small trial of your own

The best evidence about a tool is how it does on your own work. A small trial is enough to start: one easy task, one typical task, one unusual case, and one request with missing or conflicting information. Give each tool you compare the same inputs and the same idea of a good result, then note what worked, what didn't, what help it needed and how long the whole thing took, including your checking.

Four tasks won't prove that a tool is reliable in general. They show quickly whether it fits your work. Chapter 5 covers how to weigh the result against its cost.

Try this

Build your four-task trial from real work. Run it on the tool you use now and on one alternative, with the same inputs, and note how long you spent checking each result.

Words you'll meet in this guide

Model, or large language model
The system behind an AI app, trained on huge amounts of text to predict what comes next.
Chat app
An app where you talk to a model in everyday language, such as ChatGPT or Claude.
Prompt
What you ask or tell the AI.
Context
Everything the AI can see while it works: your prompt, the conversation so far, the files you've shared and the results from its tools.
Multimodal
Able to work with more than one kind of media, such as text, images, sound and video.
Token
A small chunk of text, often part of a word. Models read and write in tokens, and many apps charge by them.
Hallucination
A confident answer that's false or made up.
Agent
AI that takes actions to finish a task, such as searching, writing files, running code or using apps.
Skill
Saved instructions an agent can load for a particular kind of task.
MCP
The Model Context Protocol, a common standard for connecting AI to other apps and data.
Computer use
An agent operating a computer's screen, mouse and keyboard, the way a person would.
Benchmark
A standard test used to compare AI models.

Check your understanding

You ask a chat app for a fact, and it answers confidently, naming a source. What do you do before relying on it?

Check it. Models can state false information fluently, and asking again can repeat the same mistake. Open the source, or check the fact somewhere you trust.

Which of these is fine to paste into a public AI chat app?

The public article. Keep passwords, ID and bank numbers, other people's personal details and confidential work data out of chat apps.

A leaderboard puts one model first. What should you check before switching to it?

What was tested and how: the task, the model version, the tools it had and how many tries it took. Then try it on a few of your own tasks.
