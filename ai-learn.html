Using AI, chapter 1 of 6

Learn what AI can do

About 9 minutes to read

You don't need to know everything about AI before you start. A broad map of what exists, a few good places to keep up, and the habit of testing tools on your own work will take you further than any ranking.

Key ideas

  • Learn a little of everything first, then deepen it by using AI on real tasks.
  • Keep up through practitioners and the labs' own announcements, and learn by teaching others.
  • Judge what AI can do from work samples: the task, the data, the mistakes and the fixes.
  • The model is one part of a product; tools, memory, skills and connections change what it can do.
  • Benchmarks help you shortlist. A small trial on your own work tells you what fits.

Learn a little of everything, then practise

The most useful start is a broad, high-level understanding: a little of everything, kept in mind, then deepened as you use it. You don't need to study how models are trained before you can use one well. Here's a map, from the basics to optional depth:

  1. How models work, at a high level. Tokens, the context window, and what "multimodal" means (working with text, images and sound). This explains what the model sees, and why long tasks cost more and can run out of room.
  2. Where AI is used. The sectors and tasks it already serves, which gives you a rough sense of what it can do.
  3. The products. Chat apps such as Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, DeepSeek, Doubao, Qwen and Kimi; agentic coding apps from the same companies and others, such as GitHub Copilot; and web builders such as Lovable. Learn which companies train their own models and which build on someone else's, and how each is priced.
  4. Working techniques. The prompt, the context and the loop: how you brief the AI, what it can see, and how its work gets checked. Chapters 2 and 3 cover these.
  5. Agent features. Skills, MCP, plugins and hooks: the parts that let an agent use tools, reach other systems and repeat a method.
  6. Optional depth. How models are trained today, and the research papers the main labs publish. Worth it if you want to go deeper, though you can work well with AI without it.

Then practise, and keep learning how to use what you know better. Before any task, ask what its steps are and which of them AI can help with.

In my practice

Before working on a task, I always ask myself what its steps are and which of them AI can help with. Practice makes the rest: I use AI across my work and personal life, from research and learning to writing and coding.

Keep up through practitioners and the labs

AI products change every few months, so where you learn matters as much as what you learn. Two kinds of source complement each other:

  • Practitioners and content creators show real use and give quick feedback on new releases, including whether published benchmark results hold up in practice.
  • The official accounts of the main AI companies, both the labs that train models and the teams behind AI apps, tell you what's new and what has changed.

Most learning still comes from use. People who use AI often know a trick you don't, and explaining AI to someone who doesn't use it shows you where your own understanding is thin.

In my practice

I learn from reading posts on X and from constantly using AI in my personal life and at work. I also talk with people who use AI, to see if there's anything new to me, and with people who don't, such as friends and colleagues, because I learn by teaching.

Learn the method behind a demo

A demo, an expert's article or a social media thread may show you something you'd never have thought of: rehearsing a difficult conversation, producing practice data for a class, or an agent turning a brief into a working prototype. Treat these as invitations to investigate. Seeing an expert do it doesn't mean the same setup will work as well for you.

A reputation gets a candidate an interview; a relevant work sample shows whether they fit. When someone says an AI is excellent at analytics, look for the task, the data it was given, the mistakes, the corrections and the finished result. A screenshot of a polished dashboard says little about whether the measures are right, or how long it took to fix.

Look for the decisions behind the demonstration. Why did the person supply those definitions? Why did they keep that step manual? What failure did their test catch? Ask what this person knows, supplies or checks that makes the method work. An expert may hand a step to AI because they can spot subtle errors in it; your version may need smaller steps or an extra check.

Common trap: copying the prompt

The wording of an expert's prompt is the least important part of their method. The definitions they supplied and the checks they kept are what made it work.

The model is only one part of the product

A chat app or a coding agent wraps the model in other parts, so the same model can perform differently in different products.5 Each part adds a capability or carries work over from one session to the next.

  • Tools let the AI act: search the web, run code, use a computer.
  • The harness is the workplace: the files, permissions, state and records that let the work actually run.5
  • Memory carries selected notes from one session to the next, like a notebook that may need correcting.6 The context is what the AI can see now; memory is one way information gets back into it later.
  • Skills package reusable instructions, resources and sometimes scripts that an agent loads when they're relevant.2 A skill keeps a good method; it can also keep a bad one, so test it first.
  • Connections let the AI reach other systems and agents. The Model Context Protocol (MCP), announced in November 2024, connects AI to external tools and data;3 Agent2Agent (A2A), announced in April 2025, is for agents talking to each other.4
  • Retrieval brings relevant documents into the task. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pairs a model with searchable information;1 it doesn't teach the model those facts for good, and a retrieved passage still has to support the claim it's used for.

When a result disappoints, the model is just one of the parts to suspect. The brief, the context, a missing tool or permission, or a weak test are often the cause, as chapter 3 sets out.

Read AI's history as a branching map

Knowing the history matters because you can't consider help you don't know exists. A common story runs from web chat to web agent to coding agent to memory to cloud assistant. It mixes different kinds of change: a website or terminal is an interface, coding is a domain, memory is a capability, and the cloud is a location. None of them is a level of intelligence, and coding agents didn't simply come after web agents.

Read benchmarks like an analyst

A leaderboard invites one question: who's first? A more useful one is: what exactly was measured? Anyone who works with KPIs knows a headline number depends on its definition, population and aggregation. Apply the same discipline here.

CapabilityExample evaluationWhat it doesn't tell you
Specialist knowledgeGPQA: hard science questions18Professional competence in every field
Software changesSWE-bench: fixing issues in real code repositories19Whether it completes your whole project
Computer and tool useOSWorld and BFCL: tasks on a computer, calls to tools2021Safe, correct action in every app

What people preferArena: people comparing answers side by side23Whether the answers are factually right
Speed and priceArtificial Analysis: quality, speed and cost24Your own cost of reviewing and fixing

Then check the setup. Which model and version was tested? What tools did it have? Is the score for the model alone or for a whole agent system? Did it take several attempts? A result after several tries says little about getting it right first time, and agent evaluations treat the task, the attempts and the grading as part of the evidence.25 A benchmark helps you shortlist; a work sample tells you whether it fits.

In my practice

I check Artificial Analysis to see which models are good at what, and I follow practitioners to see whether benchmark results hold up in real use. When a benchmark or other users disagree with my experience of a tool, I try it with and without their feedback before disagreeing. If I still disagree, I keep their view in mind, carry on with my current tool, and research the real use cases behind it.

Run a small trial of your own

The best evidence about a tool is how it does on your own work. A small trial set is enough to start: one straightforward task, one typical task, one edge case, and one request with missing or conflicting information. Keep the inputs and the success criteria the same when you compare setups, and record what passed, what failed, what help was needed and the total effort, including your checking.

Four tasks won't prove that a tool is reliable in general. They show quickly whether it fits your work. Chapter 5, still in progress, covers how to weigh the result against its cost.

Try this

Build your four-task trial set this week from real work. Run it on the tool you use now and on one alternative, with the same inputs, and note how long you spent checking each result.

Check your understanding

A leaderboard puts one model first. What should you check before switching?

What was measured and how: the task, the model version, the tools it had, whether the score is for the model alone or a whole agent system, and how many attempts it took. Then try it on a few of your own tasks.

Someone shares a polished dashboard an AI built. What would tell you the AI is good at this?

The work behind it: the data it was given, the definitions, the mistakes and corrections, and how long it took to get right.

An agent forgets a decision you made in yesterday's session. Which part of the product is involved?

Memory. The decision has to be carried over in memory or put back into the context, for example in a project notes file the agent reads at the start.
