Using AI

Working with a collaborator you've just met

A practical guide, in six chapters, to getting work from AI that you can trust. It draws on how I use AI as a business intelligence analyst and in everyday life.

Think of AI as a capable collaborator you've only just met

Imagine hiring someone, at a modest cost, who can discuss an extraordinary range of subjects. They can draft, explain, analyse and help you build things. But you've only just met. You don't yet know where their abilities end, how much context they need, or which parts of their work deserve a close look.

That's a useful way to approach AI: a broadly capable collaborator whose reliability you establish task by task. It's a comparison for a working relationship; AI isn't a person.

Two gaps stand between you and a useful result

Every piece of work with AI starts with two gaps. You don't fully know what it can do, and that gap closes with work samples and checking. It doesn't know your situation, and that gap closes with a useful brief and the right context.

Neither gap closes because an answer sounds professional: language models can state false information fluently and confidently. What you're after is a worthwhile outcome you can trust.

How much you know about the subject changes how you work together. In a subject you know well, you can direct the work like a manager: set the purpose, spot the assumptions that matter and judge whether the result answers the right question. In an unfamiliar subject, you're closer to an apprentice: ask the AI to explain its choices, try a small part yourself, and arrange checks you don't have to judge alone. Most real tasks mix both, so expect to switch roles within one piece of work.

Speed pays off only when it turns into value

Most talk about AI at work is about speed: drafting faster, coding faster, answering faster. Speed matters, but on its own it doesn't show up in revenue or in the KPIs a business cares about. A task finished in half the time creates value only when the time saved goes into something that matters.

The way I see it, if a company pays for AI and people finish the same work faster, but the time saved goes into nothing more, the company is paying more for the same results. The return comes when people use AI to take on work they couldn't reach before: a deeper analysis, a new idea tested, a long-standing problem fixed.

So the question worth asking is what more valuable work AI lets you take on. Doing today's tasks faster is the starting point.

Set against people who don't use AI at all, the picture is clearer still. Using AI without redirecting the time saved ends up close to not using it, while paying for it:

Everyone has a part in this. People who use AI to create more value make themselves more valuable to their teams. Leaders have a part too: when work gets faster, someone has to decide what the freed time goes to. Without that, money spent on AI mostly makes the same work easier.

There's a limit worth planning for as well. Productivity can rise faster than the value it creates: people do more, but the results barely move. When that happens, organisations start looking for savings elsewhere, which is hard on people and, if it happens widely, weighs on the wider economy. The better path is to keep pointing the gains at work that grows the business, so that more output becomes more value. The guide's chapter 5 looks at judging that value in practice.
